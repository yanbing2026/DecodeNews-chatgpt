美国中期选举如何计票？从首批结果到正式认证可能相隔数周

美国2026年中期选举于11月3日举行，但投票结束并不意味着当晚就能知道所有赢家。选票由州和地方选举机构管理，各地对提前投票、邮寄选票、临时选票、签名补正和结果认证有不同规定。首批数字通常会在投票站关闭后陆续公布；竞争激烈的选区可能需要数小时、数天，甚至更久才能确定胜者。美国选举援助委员会（EAC）提醒，选举夜公布的数字属于初步结果，即使页面显示所有投票区均已报告，也不等于正式结果。

## 选票先经过分类和核验

选民可能在提前投票期间现场投票、选举日到投票站投票，或通过邮寄方式投票。地方选举工作人员会接收选票，核对选民资格及选票是否符合当地规定，再将纸质选票交由计票设备统计。The Guardian指出，纸票通常由机器计数，部分地区还会进行人工抽查和审计；计票结果往往分批上传，而不是等到所有选票处理完毕后一次性公布。

邮寄选票的处理时间尤其取决于州法。有些州允许选举日邮戳符合要求的选票在选举日之后送达；另一些州要求选票必须在选举日当天或之前收到。若选票签名需要核实或补充资料，选民可能有机会在规定期限内完成补正，这类选票往往较晚才计入结果。

## 州规则决定计票快慢

各州的计票节奏并不一致。CBS News报道，宾夕法尼亚州和威斯康星州法律不允许选举工作人员在选举日早晨之前处理邮寄选票，因此即使选票已经送达，也可能无法在投票站关闭后立即反映在结果中。相反，允许提前处理邮寄选票的地方，可能较早公布这部分选票的统计结果。

Al Jazeera列出的较慢州份包括加利福尼亚、阿拉斯加、内华达和华盛顿。加州大量选民通过邮寄投票，部分选票需要在投票结束后继续接收和核验；内华达允许符合邮戳要求的邮寄选票在选举日后数日内送达，并设有签名问题补正期。阿拉斯加和缅因采用排序选择投票制度，若没有候选人在首轮计票中取得过半支持，可能还要进行后续轮次统计。Alaska的下一轮排序计票安排在11月18日。

## 媒体预测不等于官方结果

新闻机构会依据已公布的非正式计票数字，判断某位候选人是否已无法追上对手。Reuters报道，媒体使用的做法并不完全相同：Associated Press依靠地方记者收集县级数据，并估算尚未统计的选票；部分电视网会结合AP数据、出口民调、提前投票资料和自己的统计模型作出预测。Reuters还指出，Decision Desk HQ会收集州政府公布的数据，并通过团队审核作出判断。

AP说明，“预计已计票比例”本身是估算值，会随着实际投票总数和新数据更新而变化；“99%的投票区已报告”也不一定代表99%的选票已经统计完毕，因为部分地区会把提前投票集中列在一个投票区名下，或先公布某个投票区的一部分数字。因此，媒体宣布预测赢家只是新闻判断，不会停止地方选举机构继续点算选票。

## 复核、重计和认证仍在后面

所有合资格选票处理后，地方机构还要进行canvass，即核对选民人数、已投选票和计票总数是否相符，并按州法完成审计或其他检查。之后，相关选举机构才会认证最终结果。EAC表示，这一过程可能需要数天，有时甚至数周。若候选人差距非常接近，州法可能要求自动重计票，或允许候选人依法申请重计。

因此，选举夜的领先者可能随着未统计选票陆续加入而改变。领先变化本身不能证明舞弊，也不能单凭媒体预测宣布官方胜负。判断结果是否最终确定，应查看州和地方选举机构发布的认证信息，而不是只看电视画面上的预测或实时百分比。

How Are Votes Counted in the 2026 US Midterms? From First Returns to Official Certification

The 2026 US midterm elections are being held on November 3, but the close of polling places does not mean that every winner will be known that night. Elections are administered by state and local authorities, and rules differ for early voting, mail ballots, provisional ballots, signature corrections and certification. Initial figures generally appear after polling places close. Contests that are close may take hours, days or longer to resolve. The US Election Assistance Commission (EAC) emphasizes that figures released on election night are preliminary. Even a results page showing every precinct reporting does not mean that the outcome is official.

## Ballots must be sorted and verified

Voters may cast ballots in person during an early-voting period, at a polling place on Election Day, or by mail. Local election workers receive ballots, check voter eligibility and determine whether each ballot meets applicable requirements. Paper ballots are then tabulated, often using machines. The Guardian reports that machine counts may be supplemented by partial hand counts and audits. Results are commonly uploaded in batches rather than held until every ballot has been processed.

The treatment of mail ballots is particularly dependent on state law. Some states accept ballots that arrive after Election Day if they were postmarked by the deadline. Other states require ballots to be received by Election Day. A voter whose mail-ballot signature needs verification or additional information may have an opportunity to correct the problem within a state-set period. Those ballots can be among the last to be included in reported totals.

## State rules shape the counting timetable

There is no single national timetable for counting ballots. CBS News reports that Pennsylvania and Wisconsin do not allow election officials to begin processing mail ballots until the morning of Election Day. As a result, ballots that have already arrived may not be reflected in the first results released after polls close. States that permit earlier processing may be able to report those ballots sooner.

Al Jazeera identifies California, Alaska, Nevada and Washington among the states where final results may take longer. California relies heavily on mail voting, and some ballots still need to be received and verified after Election Day. Nevada allows certain mail ballots postmarked by Election Day to arrive during the following days and provides a period for voters to correct signature problems. Alaska and Maine use ranked-choice voting. If no candidate receives a majority in the initial count, additional rounds may be needed. Alaska's next ranked-choice tabulation is scheduled for November 18.

These differences mean that a slower count is not necessarily evidence of an administrative problem. The number of ballots still outstanding, the size of the electorate, the closeness of a contest and the state's procedures all affect when the picture becomes clear.

## Media projections are not official results

News organizations analyze unofficial returns to determine whether a trailing candidate can still catch the apparent leader. Reuters reports that major outlets do not all use the same method. The Associated Press gathers county-level results through local reporters and estimates how many votes remain outstanding. Some television networks combine AP vote totals with exit polls, early-voting information and their own statistical models before projecting a winner. Reuters also reports that Decision Desk HQ gathers figures from official state sources and subjects its projections to internal review.

The AP cautions that its estimate of the percentage of expected votes counted is not a fixed official figure. It can change as turnout estimates are refined and new returns arrive. A report that 99% of precincts have reported also does not necessarily mean that 99% of ballots have been counted. In some jurisdictions, early votes may be grouped under a single precinct, or a precinct may initially report only part of its total. Additional ballots can therefore be added even when the precinct-reporting percentage looks nearly complete.

A media organization calling a race is making a projection based on the information available to its decision desk. That projection does not stop election officials from counting every eligible ballot. Nor does it replace the official process by which election authorities certify the result.

## Canvasses, recounts and certification come later

After eligible ballots have been processed, local authorities conduct a canvass. This involves reconciling the number of voters with the ballots cast and checking that reported totals are complete and consistent. States may also conduct audits or other reviews before certifying the result. The EAC says this process can take several days and sometimes weeks.

A very narrow margin may trigger an automatic recount under state law, or a candidate may be able to request one under specified conditions. A recount is different from an audit: it involves counting ballots again and can change the official tally. The rules and deadlines vary by jurisdiction.

For these reasons, the candidate who leads in early returns may lose that lead as outstanding ballots are added. A changing margin, by itself, is not proof of fraud. Likewise, a media projection should not be confused with a certified result. Voters seeking the final outcome should look to the relevant state and local election authorities, which are responsible for completing the canvass and issuing official certification.
