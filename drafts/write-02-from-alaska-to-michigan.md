# 从阿拉斯加到密歇根：两场参议院选战牵动国会控制权

## 两州争议放大参院版图
美国中期选举进入最后阶段，阿拉斯加与密歇根的参议院选战接连出现意外，成为两党争夺参议院多数席位的关键变量。NBC News分析指出，民主党即使守住自己正在争取保住的席位，并在北卡罗来纳州和缅因州取得进展，仍可能需要在特朗普2024年大幅领先的州中再赢下多个席位。阿拉斯加因此不只是地方选举，也可能影响全国参议院控制权。

## 密歇根出现党内倒戈
密歇根州民主党参议员候选人Abdul El-Sayed的前初选对手、众议员Haley Stevens在投票日前两天公开支持共和党候选人Mike Rogers。美联社报道，Stevens此前曾支持民主党提名人，但在与El-Sayed阵营关系恶化后，转而与Rogers同台。她的决定令民主党内部团结问题在选举最后关头再次成为焦点。

## El-Sayed转向催票动员
Stevens倒戈后，El-Sayed与密歇根州民主党人士试图把注意力重新拉回投票动员。Reuters报道，El-Sayed阵营称，Stevens宣布改挺Rogers当天，竞选团队获得了240万美元捐款，创下该团队单日筹款纪录。Rogers则继续把El-Sayed描述为立场过于激进；El-Sayed呼吁不同背景的选民团结。密歇根选情的影响不止于州内，也关系到民主党能否扩大参议院席位。

## 阿拉斯加出现同名候选人
阿拉斯加的争议围绕共和党现任参议员Dan S. Sullivan，以及同样以Dan J. Sullivan之名参选的退休教师展开。The New York Times报道，民主党阵营相关人士曾参与招募可能分流现任参议员选票的候选人；Mary Peltola否认自己参与相关安排。Dan J. Sullivan则曾表示，参选是自己的决定。阿拉斯加采用排序选择投票，选票上也以党派和现任身份等信息区分两名候选人。

## 法院驳回联邦介入请求
特朗普政府司法部曾要求法院阻止阿拉斯加在现任参议员Dan S. Sullivan落败时认证选举结果，并希望隔离涉及Dan J. Sullivan的选票。联邦地区法官Sharon Gleason驳回诉讼。CNN报道，法官认为联邦政府没有资格强制州方隔离选票；她还指出，这种做法会危及选民秘密投票权。司法部随后提出上诉。驳回诉讼并不等于法院认定有关招募行为不存在，而是对政府要求采取的法律措施作出否定。

## 小镇调查与选举余波
美联社走访阿拉斯加渔业小镇Petersburg时发现，FBI探员已询问为Dan J. Sullivan捐款的居民。当地居民对调查看法不一：有人认为联邦执法行动过度，也有人认为招募同名候选人不妥。司法部正在调查相关指控，但报道指出，目前没有竞选相关人士因此被刑事起诉。指控、调查与已被证实的违法行为必须区分。

## 特朗普的最后竞选讯息
投票日前夕，特朗普在华盛顿林肯纪念堂倒影池发表讲话，重点介绍整修工程，并批评民主党候选人。他承认执政党通常难以在中期选举中获胜，同时敦促支持者投票。NBC News将这场讲话视为特朗普的选举前最后主要公开讯息。密歇根的党内分裂、阿拉斯加的法律争议，以及选民对经济和执政表现的判断，最终都要由选票决定；阿拉斯加的邮寄选票与排序选择计票程序也意味着结果可能不会立即明朗。

# From Alaska to Michigan, Senate Candidates Face Critical Stakes in Closing Messages

## Two contests loom over Senate control
As the 2026 midterm campaign reached its final hours, unexpected developments in Alaska and Michigan highlighted how a small number of Senate contests could shape control of Congress. NBC News described the two states as important pieces in the fight for a Senate majority. Democrats’ path depends not only on defending seats but also on converting competitive races and making gains in states that backed Donald Trump by wide margins in the 2024 presidential election. Alaska, in particular, could become decisive if the national balance remains close.

## Michigan sees a dramatic party break
Michigan’s Senate race was jolted when Democratic Rep. Haley Stevens, who had lost the party’s primary to Abdul El-Sayed, endorsed Republican nominee Mike Rogers just two days before Election Day. The Associated Press reported that Stevens appeared with Rogers after tensions with El-Sayed’s campaign had escalated. Her move was an extraordinary public break with the party’s nominee at a moment when Democrats were trying to maintain unity and turn out voters. It also gave Republicans an opportunity to emphasize divisions among Democrats in a closely watched contest.

## El-Sayed turns back to voter turnout
El-Sayed and other Michigan Democrats sought to move beyond the endorsement and refocus the campaign on getting supporters to the polls. Reuters reported that El-Sayed’s campaign said it raised $2.4 million on Sunday, the day Stevens endorsed Rogers, describing it as the campaign’s largest single-day fundraising total. Rogers continued to characterize El-Sayed as too extreme, while El-Sayed appealed for unity across differences in geography, race, gender identity and faith. The race matters beyond Michigan: its outcome could affect the arithmetic of the Senate majority.

## A same-name candidate unsettles Alaska
In Alaska, Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan is facing Democrat Mary Peltola and another candidate named Dan J. Sullivan, a retired teacher from the fishing community of Petersburg. The New York Times reported that political operatives aligned with Peltola’s campaign had worked to recruit candidates who could draw votes away from the incumbent. Peltola has denied personal involvement in the alleged effort, while Dan J. Sullivan has said he entered the race on his own. The state’s ranked-choice voting system and ballot labels distinguishing the incumbent from the challenger add important context to the dispute.

## Judge rejects the federal intervention bid
The Justice Department sought to prevent Alaska from certifying the Senate result if the incumbent narrowly lost, and asked for ballots associated with Dan J. Sullivan to be segregated. US District Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed the lawsuit. CNN reported that she found the federal government lacked standing to force the state to separate ballots and said the proposed process would threaten voters’ right to cast a secret ballot. The Justice Department filed an appeal. The ruling rejected the remedy the government sought; it did not resolve every factual allegation about how the challenger was recruited or establish that anyone committed a crime.

## A small town drawn into the investigation
An Associated Press report from Petersburg described FBI agents questioning residents who had donated to Dan J. Sullivan’s campaign. The community’s response was divided. Some residents viewed the federal inquiry as excessive, while others considered the alleged recruitment tactic improper. Federal authorities are investigating allegations that Democratic campaigners sought to recruit candidates to run against the incumbent. The reporting did not establish that the people involved had been charged with a crime. An investigation, an allegation and a criminal finding are different things, and the distinction is especially important in a heated election.

## Trump delivers his final campaign message
On the eve of voting, Trump spoke at the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool in Washington, emphasizing renovation work and criticizing Democratic candidates. He acknowledged that the president’s party historically tends to struggle in midterm elections, while urging supporters to vote. NBC News characterized the remarks as his final major public speech before Election Day. The closing arguments in Michigan and Alaska unfolded against a broader national debate over the economy, the administration’s record and the future direction of Congress. Alaska’s mail-ballot and ranked-choice counting procedures also mean that the final result may take longer to settle than the election-night vote totals suggest.
