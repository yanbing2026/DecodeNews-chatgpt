美国中期选举投票日开启：国会控制权之争进入最后阶段

美国选民11月3日开始投票，决定众议院、参议院以及多个州政府的席位归属。这场中期选举被广泛视为对总统Donald Trump第二任期前两年政策的检验。民调和分析人士普遍认为民主党更有机会夺回众议院，但参议院竞争仍难预测。投票日开始前，已有超过5700万选民提前投票，选举结果可能影响白宫未来两年的立法空间、预算谈判和调查监督。

## 提前投票规模已超过5700万

CBS News和ABC News援引University of Florida Election Lab的数据称，截至11月2日下午，全国已有超过5700万选民提前投票。这个数字已接近2022年中期选举超过5800万的提前投票总量。提前投票人数增加并不必然意味着最终投票率同比例上升，因为部分选民只是改变投票时间或方式；最终参与程度仍要等选举结束后统计。

各州投票时间不同。CBS News指出，部分州投票站清晨开放，其他州会在当地时间稍后开放。选民若在投票站关闭前已经排队，仍可依当地规定完成投票。各州邮寄选票接收期限、选民资格核验和临时选票处理规则也不相同，因此初步结果公布速度可能存在差异。

## 众议院和参议院门槛不同

本次众议院435个席位全部改选，参议院100席中有35席进入选举。Reuters报道，民主党至少需要净增3个众议院席位、净增4个参议院席位，才能分别取得两院多数。民主党在众议院的胜算相对较高，但参议院战场分散在多个共和党占优的州，任何一两场关键选举都可能改变整体结果。

AP指出，民主党希望同时控制两院，以限制Trump政府的政策推进；但重新划分选区、城乡政治分化以及竞争选区数量有限，可能限制民主党的席位增长。参议院方面，Georgia、North Carolina等东部州的较早结果可能为全国走势提供线索，Ohio、Texas、Alaska、Kansas和Maine等州的竞争则可能持续到较晚时间。

## 关键州选举与政治争议

Reuters重点关注Michigan、Alaska、Texas、Kansas和Maine等参议院选战。Michigan民主党候选人Abdul El-Sayed与共和党候选人Mike Rogers的竞争，受到跨党派背书和身份政治争议影响。Alaska选举则因第三名候选人与共和党现任参议员Dan Sullivan同名而引发争议；Trump政府司法部提出的阻止认证诉讼已被法官驳回。相关指控和法律行动仍应与法院已经作出的裁定区分。

AP报道，选举监督也是投票日焦点。司法部表示，将派出约1000名工作人员和观察员前往全美50个州，关注选民投票权和可能的选民恐吓问题。联邦政府称此举是为确保联邦选举法律得到遵守；选举监督人员和民间组织则担忧政府资源可能被用于干预投票。现阶段应把已经发生的部署、各方提出的担忧以及尚未发生的干预行为分开描述。

## 结果公布可能不会在当晚结束

投票站关闭后，各州会陆续公布非正式计票结果，但邮寄选票、临时选票和接近的票差可能使部分选区需要更长时间才能确定胜者。The Guardian和Al Jazeera均提醒，部分州的最终结果可能要到数日后才明朗。媒体预测某位候选人胜出，并不等同于州政府完成计票和认证。

投票日的核心问题不仅是哪个党控制国会，也包括选民对生活成本、伊朗战争及能源价格、移民和政府治理的态度是否转化为选票。最终结果将决定Trump政府在国会面临的制衡程度，但在官方认证完成之前，实时领先数字仍应视为暂定信息。

US Midterm Election Day Begins as Battle for Control of Congress Enters Its Final Stage

Americans began voting on November 3 in elections that will determine control of the House of Representatives, part of the Senate and a number of state governments. The midterms are widely viewed as a test of President Donald Trump's performance during the first two years of his second term. Polls and nonpartisan analysts generally give Democrats a better chance of retaking the House, while the Senate remains much less predictable. More than 57 million voters had already cast ballots before Election Day, and the outcome could shape the White House's room to legislate, negotiate budgets and conduct oversight during the next two years.

## Early voting has topped 57 million

CBS News and ABC News, citing the University of Florida Election Lab, reported that more than 57 million Americans had voted early as of Monday afternoon, November 2. That figure was approaching the more than 58 million early votes cast during the 2022 midterm elections. A larger early-voting total does not automatically mean that overall turnout will rise by the same proportion. Some voters may simply be changing when or how they cast their ballots. The final participation rate will not be known until the election process is further along.

Polling hours vary by state and locality. CBS News reported that some polling places opened early in the morning, while others were scheduled to open later according to local time. Voters who are already in line when a polling place closes are generally entitled to remain and cast a ballot under applicable rules. States also differ in their deadlines for receiving mail ballots, procedures for verifying voter eligibility and handling of provisional ballots. Those differences can affect how quickly preliminary results become available.

## The House and Senate have different paths to a majority

All 435 seats in the House are being contested, along with 35 of the Senate's 100 seats. Reuters reported that Democrats need a net gain of at least three House seats and four Senate seats to win majorities in both chambers. The party's path to the House appears more favorable, but the Senate map is spread across several states where Republicans have traditionally held an advantage. A small number of closely contested races could determine control of the chamber.

The Associated Press said Democrats hope to control both chambers and use that position to constrain Trump's policy agenda. However, redrawn congressional districts, the sharp political divide between urban and rural voters and a relatively limited number of competitive seats could restrict the size of Democratic gains. In the Senate, early returns from states such as Georgia and North Carolina may provide clues about the national picture. Contests in Ohio, Texas, Alaska, Kansas and Maine could remain unresolved later into the night or beyond.

## Battleground states and political disputes

Reuters highlighted Senate contests in Michigan, Alaska, Texas, Kansas and Maine. The Michigan race between Democrat Abdul El-Sayed and Republican Mike Rogers has drawn attention because of a cross-party endorsement and disputes involving identity politics. In Alaska, the campaign has been complicated by allegations surrounding a third candidate who shares a name with Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan. The Trump administration's Justice Department filed a lawsuit seeking to block certification, but a judge dismissed that effort. Allegations and legal actions should be distinguished from findings that a court has actually made.

Election oversight is another major issue on Election Day. The AP reported that the Justice Department said it would deploy about 1,000 department staff and observers across all 50 states, focusing on voter access and possible intimidation. The department said the deployment was intended to ensure compliance with federal election laws. Election-protection groups, meanwhile, have expressed concern about the possibility that federal resources could be used to influence voting. The reported deployment, concerns raised by outside groups and any actual interference are separate matters and should not be conflated.

## Results may not be settled on election night

After polling places close, states will release unofficial vote totals in stages. Mail ballots, provisional ballots and narrow margins can mean that some contests take longer to resolve. The Guardian and Al Jazeera both cautioned that final results in some states may take several days. A media organization projecting a winner is not the same as a state completing its count and certifying the result.

The central question on Election Day is not only which party will control Congress. Voters are also delivering a judgment on affordability, the war with Iran and energy prices, immigration and the performance of government. The final outcome will determine how much congressional oversight and opposition the Trump administration faces during the second half of its term. Until state and local authorities complete the required counting and certification procedures, live leads and projections should be treated as provisional rather than official results.
